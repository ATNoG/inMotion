\section{Related Work}

Automatic passenger counting is a well-studied problem in public transport. Video-based \ac{APC} systems deploy cameras at vehicle doors and rely on neural classifiers for detection and tracking \cite{pronello2023evaluating}. All of these approaches require dedicated hardware installed and maintained on every vehicle. Wi-Fi-based sensing is an alternative that reuses infrastructure already present on public transport. Access points passively observe the radio emissions of passenger devices, requiring no cooperation and capturing no imagery. This makes Wi-Fi sensing a privacy-preserving complement to camera-based counting.

Wi-Fi sensing operates on two distinct signal representations. Deep learning has advanced Wi-Fi-based human sensing substantially \cite{ahmad2024wifisensing}. Channel State Information (CSI) captures per-subcarrier amplitude and phase and supports fine-grained tasks such as passenger counting in bus cabins \cite{wang2024csi}. Yet CSI is only obtainable through firmware and driver modifications that most commercial access points do not expose. The \ac{RSSI}, in contrast, is available on virtually all commodity Wi-Fi hardware. RSSI fingerprinting has been studied extensively for indoor localization \cite{singh2021rssisurvey}. Probe-based systems count passengers from the identifiers broadcast by client devices \cite{nitti2020iabacus} and infer occupancy from Wi-Fi signatures \cite{myrvoll2017counting}. Ambient RSSI alone supports frictionless activity recognition \cite{sigg2014telepathic}. These systems demonstrate the viability of RSSI sensing while relying on hand-crafted features and classical classifiers.

Integrated sensing and communications treats the communications infrastructure itself as a sensing platform \cite{liu2022isac,gonzalezprelcic2024isac}. Networks that already carry data can simultaneously sense their environment, and commodity Wi-Fi is the most opportunistic instantiation of this principle. Access points become dual-function devices that provide connectivity and act as passive sensors at no additional hardware cost. Machine learning is central to the ISAC processing chain \cite{luong2025mlisac}, providing detection, localization, and activity classification from sensed radio data. Our work follows this opportunistic paradigm. Off-the-shelf access points on public transport vehicles are repurposed as passive sensors that classify passenger movements from RSSI time series, without dedicated sensing hardware and without modifying the Wi-Fi stack.

Deep sequence models are now standard tools for time-series classification. Mamba introduced selective state space models that process long sequences in linear time \cite{gu2023mamba}, and the Mamba-3 family extends this line with multi-scale sequence processing \cite{gu2026mamba3}. In parallel, self-supervised learning has progressed through joint-embedding predictive architectures. I-JEPA learns semantic representations by predicting embeddings of masked input regions \cite{assran2023ijepa}. LeJEPA adds a sliced Gaussian regularizer that prevents representational collapse \cite{balestriero2025lejepa}. JEPA variants tailored to tabular and time-series data have been proposed \cite{thimonier2024tjepa,tsjepa2024,cfjepa2026}. Self-supervised learning has been applied to Wi-Fi sensing, where collecting labeled data is costly \cite{radwan2025sslwifi}. Prior work on RSSI-based movement classification in public transport relied on classical machine learning and reached an \ac{MCC} of 0.756 with a Gaussian process classifier on the combined datasets \cite{ribeiro2026eucnc}. No previous study applies self-supervised world models to RSSI-based sensing, and this paper addresses that gap.
